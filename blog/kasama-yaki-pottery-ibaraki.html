Kasama-yaki: Ibaraki’s pottery town with no rules

by Kaho · Ibaraki, Japan

Cold days are cup-of-tea days. And if you want a nice cup from Ibaraki, go to Kasama. This small city in central Ibaraki is a pottery town. The pottery made there is called Kasama-yaki (Kasama ware).

How did Kasama-yaki start?

It started in the An’ei era of the Edo period (1772–1781). A village head in Hakoda (now part of Kasama City), Kuno Hanemon, started making pottery with Choemon, a potter from Shigaraki in Shiga Prefecture. The kiln got protection from the Kasama Domain. In 1992, Kasama-yaki was named a traditional craft of Japan.

What does Kasama-yaki look like?

The local clay has iron in it, so it turns reddish-black if you leave it plain. Old pieces often use glazes like persimmon, white or black. But here is the fun part: after World War II, the old rules about shape and style went away. Anyone could come and make pottery. So today Kasama-yaki has almost no fixed style.

In Kasama, the only rule is: make what you love.

One potter told a Kasama visitor guide there are “no binding rules” for what Kasama ware must be. Today there are about 200 kilns, artists and shops in the area, and some say more than 300 potters.

What can you do in Kasama?

  • Walk around Kasama Craft Hills, a hillside area with pottery shops and a workshop where you can try making pottery.
  • Visit the Ibaraki Ceramic Art Museum in Kasama Art Forest Park.
  • Rent a bicycle at Kasama Station and look at galleries.
  • Eat soba. Kasama is known for soba restaurants, too.

When is the best time to visit?

Any season is OK, but the biggest event is the Kasama Himatsuri pottery festival during Golden Week (April 29 to May 5), with more than 200 potters selling from tents. There is also a New Year pottery market called Irodori Hatsugama Ichi. Check the city website for dates before you go.

How to get to Kasama

Kasama Station is on the JR Mito Line, west of Mito. From Tokyo, take the JR Joban Line and change to the Mito Line. By car, it is around an hour and a half from Tokyo. Across the mountains is Mashiko in Tochigi, another pottery town, and its pottery has roots in Kasama. People call the two together “Kasamashiko.”
