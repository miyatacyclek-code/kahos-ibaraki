Japan’s Thanksgiving has no turkey: Kinro Kansha no Hi

by Kaho · Ibaraki, Japan

In late November, many of my friends overseas start talking about turkey and pumpkin pie. Japan has a Thanksgiving too, and it is only two days later than you might think: November 23. But if you visit a Japanese home that day, you will not find a turkey.

The holiday is called Kinro Kansha no Hi (勤労感謝の日). It means “Labor Thanksgiving Day.” In 2026 it falls on Monday, November 23, so many people get a three-day weekend.

Thank you for work

The idea is simple: we thank people for their work, and we thank each other. The 1948 law that made the holiday says it is a day to respect work, celebrate what we produce, and give thanks to one another.

So the day is quiet. There is no special meal and no big party. Many children make thank-you cards or drawings for their parents, or help with cooking and chores. Some school kids bring cards to police officers, firefighters or hospital staff in their town.

It started with rice

This day is much older than 1948. Before the war, November 23 was Niinamesai (新嘗祭), a harvest festival. The Emperor offers the year’s new rice to the gods and gives thanks for a good harvest. He still does this ceremony every year on the same day, and many shrines around Japan hold their own Niinamesai too.

After World War II, the festival was taken out of the list of national holidays, and the day got its new name. But the heart of it stayed the same: be thankful for the food and for the people who work for it.

Why it feels right in Ibaraki

Here in the countryside, the rice fields are already cut and quiet by late November. Rice is the number one farm product in Ibaraki by value, so the new rice, shinmai, is everywhere now. You see bags of it at roadside stations and supermarkets. Eating a bowl of fresh shinmai is a very Japanese way to say “thank you for this year.”

Tips for visitors

Most shops, restaurants and sightseeing spots are open on November 23, but banks and city offices are closed. Because it is a long weekend and autumn leaves season, popular places can be busy, so go early in the morning.

If you are near a shrine, look for a Niinamesai event. And try shinmai with a little salt or natto. It is the simplest Thanksgiving dinner in the world.
