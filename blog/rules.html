Unwritten rules of the Japanese countryside

by Kaho · Ibaraki, Japan

Living in rural Japan comes with rules that are not written anywhere. Nobody explains them to you. But everybody knows them, and everybody notices when you break them. I grew up with these, so here is my guide for anyone who moves to (or visits) the Japanese countryside.

1. Say hello to everyone

In Tokyo, you don't talk to strangers. In the countryside, you say “ohayo gozaimasu” or “konnichiwa” to everyone you pass. Even people you don't know. Especially old people. A small bow is enough. If you skip it, people will think you are cold or rude.

2. Bring back omiyage

When you travel, you bring back omiyage (souvenirs) for your coworkers and sometimes your neighbors. Usually it is a box of individually wrapped snacks from the place you visited. It's a way of saying “sorry I was away, and thank you.”

3. Join the neighborhood association

Most areas have a jichikai (neighborhood association). Members take turns with jobs like collecting fees, cleaning the garbage station and passing the kairanban — a clipboard with local news that goes from house to house. There are also cleaning days for the park or the roadside. Officially they are not required. Really… they are not really optional 😅

4. Follow the garbage rules perfectly

Each type of garbage has its own day and its own place. Put it out in the morning of the right day, not the night before. A wrong bag gets a warning sticker and stays there for everyone to see. In a small town, people may even know whose bag it is.

5. Return favors — but not too fast

If someone gives you vegetables, you give something back later. Not right away (that feels like paying), and not something too expensive (that makes them feel they owe you again). Something small, a few days later, is perfect. This is called okaeshi.

6. Respect fields and private land

Don't park in front of someone's field or on a farm road. Don't walk into rice fields to take photos. Farmers need those roads, and they will find you.

7. Everybody knows everybody

In a small town, news travels fast. Your new car, your visitors, what time you come home. It can feel a bit much. But it also means people notice when an old neighbor hasn't come out for a few days, and they go check.

It sounds strict, but it's how people take care of each other.

Tips if you move to rural Japan

  • When you move in, visit your close neighbors with a small gift and introduce yourself.
  • Ask your neighbors or the city office for the garbage calendar on day one.
  • Join local events like festivals and cleaning days. It's the fastest way to be accepted.
  • Smile and greet. Even if your Japanese is not perfect, people really appreciate it.
