The pink museum for one pickle (Tochigi)

by Kaho · Ibaraki, Japan

In Japan, a pickle can have its own museum.

Today I went to Tochigi, the prefecture next to my home in rural Ibaraki. I went with my sister-in-law — my brother's wife. We became close recently, and now we often go out together on weekends. In Japanese, a sister-in-law is giri no ane or giri no imouto (義理の姉・妹). “Giri” means duty, so it sounds like “a sister by duty.” But for me, she is just a friend I want to spend my weekends with.

Our first stop: the Iwashita New Ginger Museum (岩下の新生姜ミュージアム). From the outside it looks normal. Inside, everything is pink.

What is shinshoga?

Shinshoga (新生姜) means “new ginger” — young ginger, softer and less spicy. Iwashita Foods from Tochigi started selling it pickled in 1987. Fresh new ginger is pickled with salt and acid, and it turns a soft pink by itself. The pink is the ginger itself!

Pickles in Japanese life

Tsukemono (漬物), Japanese pickles, are on the table almost every day. In the countryside many families still pickle daikon, cucumbers and plums at home. So in Japan, a pickle is not a small thing. It is part of home.

What I saw

  • The Ginger Shrine (ジンジャー神社): “ginger” and “jinja” (shrine) sound almost the same. A classic Japanese pun, with a pink torii and real omikuji fortunes.
  • The wall of every package from over the years.
  • Iwashika-chan, the pink deer mascot with ginger antlers. Japan loves mascots — we call them yuru-kyara.
  • Signature walls: famous visitors sign square boards called shikishi (色紙), and shops hang them with pride.

The café: all-you-can-eat ginger

At CAFE NEW GINGER every menu item uses the ginger. I had a pink ginger soda, pink at the bottom and clear at the top. And the ginger was all-you-can-eat — tabehoudai (食べ放題)! Japan loves tabehoudai: yakiniku, sushi, cake. But pickled ginger? A first for me.

In Japan, even a small pickle can have a whole world.
