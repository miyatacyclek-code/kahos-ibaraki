Ōsōji: why all of Japan cleans house in December

by Kaho · Ibaraki, Japan

In December, Japan gets busy. Not only with parties and shopping, but with cleaning. Windows, fans, the kitchen, the space behind the fridge. This big year-end cleaning is called ōsōji (大掃除), which simply means “big cleaning.”

Many people abroad know that Japanese kids clean their own classrooms. Ōsōji is the grown-up, once-a-year version. Homes do it, offices do it, and schools do a bigger clean before the winter break.

It started as a ritual, not a chore

The idea is very old. At the imperial court in the Heian period, there was a year-end event called susuharai (煤払い), “sweeping away the soot.” Houses back then had fires inside, so soot was a real problem. But the cleaning also had a spiritual meaning: to clear away bad things before the new year.

In the Edo period, December 13 became the set day for susuharai, and the custom spread to normal homes. December 13 is also known as the day to start New Year preparations. Some temples and shrines still do a soot-sweeping ceremony in December.

Why clean before New Year?

In Japanese tradition, a god called Toshigami-sama (年神様) visits each home at New Year to bring good luck for the year. You don’t invite a guest into a dirty house. So you clean first, then put up New Year decorations like kadomatsu (pine) or shimekazari (a rope decoration) to welcome the god.

There are even small rules about the timing. Many people avoid putting up decorations on December 29, because “9” can be read “ku,” which sounds like the word for suffering. December 31 is also avoided. It is called ichiya-kazari, “one-night decorating,” and it feels rushed and impolite to the god. And on New Year’s Day, some people don’t clean at all, so they don’t sweep the good luck away.

Is ōsōji still a thing?

Yes, but it is changing. Surveys say fewer households do a full big clean now, and many people just do it in the last few days of December, after work finishes. Some people clean a little at a time from early December instead. In the countryside, where houses are bigger and families still put up New Year decorations at the gate, you will often see people busy outside in late December.

Tip for visitors

If you stay in Japan over New Year, don’t be surprised if shops sell lots of cleaning goods in December, or if your host family is too busy to go out on December 30. If you want to join in, just ask, “Can I help?” Wiping one window together is a very Japanese way to end the year.

In Japan, cleaning doesn’t just remove dust. It makes room for a new year.
