Shoes off in Japan: the genkan, the step, and the toilet slippers

by Kaho · Ibaraki, Japan

Videos about Japanese homes are everywhere right now, and one moment always gets the most comments: shoes. Rows of shoes at the door, slippers waiting inside, and then… a second pair of slippers just for the toilet.

It looks like a lot of rules. But it all comes from one simple idea.

The genkan (玄関): where outside ends

Almost every Japanese home has a genkan, a small entrance area just inside the front door. The floor there, called the tataki, is lower, usually stone or tile. Then there is a step up, the agari-kamachi. That step is the border. Below it is “outside”. Above it is “inside”.

You take off your shoes while standing on the low part, then step up in socks. Your shoes never touch the raised floor.

Why Japan does this

The main reason is cleanliness. In a traditional Japanese home, people sit, eat and even sleep close to the floor, on tatami mats or futon. Tatami is woven rush grass, and outdoor shoes would damage it and bring dirt into the place where you rest.

There is also a feeling behind it. Outside is busy and dirty. Inside is calm and clean. Taking off your shoes is a small way to say “I’m home”.

The slipper rules

Many homes, ryokan and some offices give you slippers. A few things to know:

1. No slippers on tatami. Take them off and walk in socks or bare feet.

2. Toilet slippers stay in the toilet. Many homes have a special pair inside the toilet room. Change into them, and change back when you leave. Walking out still wearing them is the classic mistake, and yes, people will notice.

3. Point your shoes to the door. After you step up, it is polite to turn your shoes so the toes face outside, a little to the side, not in the middle.

It is not only homes. Temples, some traditional restaurants, schools, clinics and even clothing store fitting rooms ask you to take your shoes off. If you see shoes lined up at an entrance, that is your sign.

Tips for visitors

Wear shoes that are easy to take off and put on. Wear clean socks without holes, because people will see them. When in doubt, watch what the person in front of you does. And if you forget once, don’t worry. A quick “sumimasen” and a smile is enough.

In the countryside, like here in Ibaraki, older houses often have a wide genkan where neighbors stand and chat without coming in. The entrance is not just for shoes. It is a little meeting place.
