Ibaraki-ben: a fun guide to the Ibaraki dialect

by Kaho · Ibaraki, Japan

Japan has many dialects, and Ibaraki has its own: Ibaraki-ben (茨城弁). People from other places sometimes say it sounds rough or angry. Don’t worry, we are not angry! It is just how we talk. Here is a small, easy guide.

Why does Ibaraki-ben sound flat?

Standard Japanese uses pitch, high and low sounds, to tell some words apart. For example, hashi can mean “bridge” or “chopsticks”. Most of Ibaraki has no pitch accent, so both words sound the same and flat. The area around Kamisu is an exception. Some dialects in Tochigi, Fukushima and Miyagi are like this too.

What does “-ppe” mean?

This is the most famous part. People add -be or -ppe to the end of a sentence. It can mean “let’s…”, “I will…” or “probably…”.

  • Nandappe? – What is it?
  • Igappe – That’s good, isn’t it?
  • Suppe – Let’s do it
  • Iku be – Let’s go

If you hear “-ppe” at the end, you are in Ibaraki.

Other sounds you may notice

Some k and t sounds become softer, like g and d. So dekiru (can do) can sound like degiru. Also, i and e sound very close. Some people even say the name of the prefecture a bit like “Ebaraki”!

Ibaraki words to know

  • Gojappe – careless, sloppy, nonsense. Maybe the most famous Ibaraki word.
  • Aonajimi – a bruise (standard Japanese: aoaza).
  • Shaanme – it can’t be helped. It sounds kind and forgiving.
  • Deresuke – a sloppy, careless person.
  • Ijiyakeru – to feel frustrated or annoyed.

Do young people still use it?

Young people speak more standard Japanese now, especially in cities. But you can still hear Ibaraki-ben from grandparents in the countryside, at farm stands and at local festivals. The prefecture even made a dialect lesson page for kids this month, so people still love it.

Tips for visitors

You don’t need to speak Ibaraki-ben. Standard Japanese or English is fine. But if you try one word, like “igappe!” when you eat something good, local people will smile, I promise.
