Sumimasen: the Japanese word for sorry, thank you and excuse me

by Kaho · Ibaraki, Japan

If you spend one day in Japan, you will hear one word again and again: sumimasen (すみません). People say it on the train, in shops, at restaurants, and when someone holds a door.

Visitors often ask: is it “sorry”? Is it “excuse me”? Is it “thank you”? The answer is… yes. All three.

Three meanings, one word

1. Excuse me. To get attention. “Sumimasen, where is the station?” In a restaurant, you can raise your hand and say “Sumimasen!” to call the staff. In small family restaurants and izakaya in the countryside, this is how almost everyone orders.

2. Sorry. For small things, like bumping into someone or being a little late.

3. Thank you. When someone does something for you, like picking up something you dropped. English speakers say “thank you”. Many Japanese people say “sumimasen” instead.

Why “sorry” can mean “thank you”

The word comes from the verb sumu (済む), which means “to finish” or “to be settled”. Sumimasen is the polite negative form. So the feeling is something like: “This is not settled.” Someone did something for me, and I cannot pay it back yet.

Japanese language experts say this way of using it started around the Edo period, and later it became the everyday word for small apologies, thanks and requests.

Behind it is a big idea in Japanese culture: try not to be a trouble to other people. When someone helps you, you are thankful, but you also feel you made them do extra work. Sumimasen holds both feelings at once.

Tips for visitors

It is the most useful word you can learn before a trip. Use it to call staff, to pass people in a crowded train, and to say a soft sorry.

You will also hear “suimasen”. It is a more casual way to say the same word, very common in spoken Japanese.

For a real apology, people use “gomen nasai” or, in business, “moushiwake gozaimasen”. And when someone gives you a gift or a big favor, “arigatou gozaimasu” is warmer. Many people say both: “Sumimasen, arigatou gozaimasu.”

In rural towns like here in Ibaraki, where neighbors help each other a lot, you hear sumimasen even more. It is a small word that keeps people close.
