Omiyage: why Japanese people bring snacks back to the office

by Kaho · Ibaraki, Japan

In many countries, a souvenir is something you buy for yourself. In Japan, we have omiyage (お土産). It is a small gift, usually local snacks, that you bring back from a trip for other people: your family, your friends, and very often your coworkers.

So after a holiday, you may see a box of sweets on an office desk with a little note. Nobody asks why. Everyone just takes one.

Where did omiyage come from?

The usual story starts with shrine trips. In the Edo period, visiting Ise Shrine was a big dream for ordinary people. Not everyone could go, so travelers brought back charms and local goods for the people at home. Later, shops around the shrine started selling local products, and the habit spread. The kanji 土産 means something like “product of the land.” That is the key: omiyage should come from the place you visited.

Why the office?

When you take days off, someone else often covers your work. Omiyage is a quiet way to say “thank you” and “sorry for the trouble” without a big speech. It is not a law, and every workplace is different. But in many offices, coming back empty-handed can feel a little awkward.

The gift is small. The message is: I thought of you while I was away.

The unwritten rules

  • Choose individually wrapped snacks, so people can take one and eat it later
  • Buy enough for everyone, plus a few extra
  • Keep it cheap and simple. A very expensive gift can make people feel they must give something back
  • Pick something from that place. Many shops sell “limited to this area” sweets

Classic omiyage from Ibaraki

If you visit Ibaraki, here are local favorites. Mito no Ume (水戸の梅) is a famous sweet from Mito: white bean paste wrapped in soft gyuhi, then rolled in red shiso leaves soaked in syrup. It is shaped after the plum blossoms of Kairakuen. Hoshiimo (dried sweet potato) is another one, and Ibaraki is famous for it. And of course, Mito natto, sometimes sold in straw. Just keep it cold!

A tip for visitors

If you are staying with a Japanese family or meeting Japanese friends, bring a small snack from your own country. It does not need to be fancy. It is one of the easiest ways to make people smile here.

Where to buy omiyage

Big train stations and airports have whole shops just for local omiyage, so it is easy to buy on the way home. In Ibaraki, try the shops at Mito Station, roadside stations (michi-no-eki), and the gift shops near famous places like Kairakuen or Oarai.

Omiyage FAQ

  • What's the difference between omiyage and a souvenir? A souvenir is often for yourself. Omiyage is mostly for other people, and it is usually food.
  • How much should I spend? It doesn't need to be expensive. For coworkers, a box of snacks that everyone can share is perfect.
  • Do I need omiyage after a short trip? Not always. But after taking days off work, many people bring something to say thank you.
