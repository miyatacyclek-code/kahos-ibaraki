Why rural Japan is full of rice fields

by Kaho · Ibaraki, Japan

“No Tokyo. No Kyoto. Just rice fields and my everyday life in Ibaraki.” I wrote that under a short video, and many people around the world watched it. Some said it looked peaceful. Some said it looked boring. And many asked the same question: why are there so many rice fields?

Rice is more than food

In Japanese, the word gohan (ご飯) means cooked rice. But it also means “a meal.” Breakfast is asa-gohan, lunch is hiru-gohan, and dinner is ban-gohan. For a long time, a meal without rice was not really a meal in Japan.

Many Japanese people also know a small word game. The kanji for rice, 米, looks like 八十八 (88) put together. People like to say that rice takes 88 steps of work. It is a play on the shape, not real history, but it shows how much work people see in one bowl.

Ibaraki: a quiet rice country

Ibaraki has the largest rice-growing area in the Kanto region, and rice is the prefecture’s biggest farm product by value. The main variety here is Koshihikari.

You can see the rice year from a car or train window. In spring, the paddies fill with water and look like mirrors. In summer, they turn bright green. In late summer and early autumn, the rice turns gold and the harvest starts. Now in November, the fields are cut short and quiet, waiting for next spring.

New rice and a thank-you day

Autumn is the season of shinmai (新米), rice from this year’s harvest. Supermarkets put up “shinmai” signs, and many people say new rice is softer and smells sweeter.

Japan even has a holiday with roots in the rice harvest. November 23 is Labor Thanksgiving Day. Before 1948, this day was Niinamesai (新嘗祭), an old ceremony where the Emperor offers the new rice to the gods. The ceremony still takes place privately at the Imperial Palace. The public holiday is now a day to thank people for their work.

Rice has been in the news, too. In 2025, a shortage pushed prices very high. In 2026 the harvest was bigger, prices went down again, and now many farmers are worried about their income. Rice is not just scenery here. It is people’s life and work.

Tips for visitors

  • Do not walk into a paddy or along the narrow paths between them. They are part of someone’s farm.
  • Ask before you take close-up photos of farmers working.
  • In autumn, try shinmai. Plain rice at a set-meal restaurant or a simple onigiri is the best way to taste it.
  • For the best views, come in May for the water mirrors, or in September for the gold fields.
