Ohitorisama: why eating alone in Japan is totally normal

by Kaho · Ibaraki, Japan

Many visitors tell me the same thing: “I was nervous to eat alone in Japan, but nobody even looked at me.” That is true. In Japan, eating alone is not strange at all. We even have a polite word for it.

When I posted a video of me eating $1 sushi, I wrote “Eating alone is totally normal here.” Many people in the comments were surprised. So let me explain.

What does ohitorisama mean?

Ohitorisama (お一人様) means “one person,” but in a very polite way. “O” and “sama” are honorific words, and “hitori” means one person. At first it was just what restaurant staff said to a guest who came alone. Later, around the 2000s, the word became popular for people who enjoy doing things by themselves: eating, traveling, karaoke, even yakiniku.

The feeling is “alone, but not lonely.” You are not waiting for anyone. You choose your own food, your own time, your own pace.

Why is it so normal here?

One big reason is simple: many people live alone. In the 2020 national census, about 38% of all households in Japan were one-person households. Students, office workers, older people. So shops are made for them.

Another reason is the food itself. Ramen, soba, gyudon, curry and sushi are often eaten at a counter. You sit, you eat, you go. At many ramen shops, you buy a ticket from a machine, so you don’t even need to talk much. Some famous ramen chains have small booths with walls on both sides, so you can focus only on your bowl.

And in Japan, people usually don’t watch other people while they eat. Everyone is in their own small world. That makes it easy to relax.

Solo dining in Ibaraki

You don’t need Tokyo for this. In Mito, conveyor-belt sushi like Kaiten Sushi Marukuni is perfect for one person: plates start around ¥150, you sit at the counter, and you take only what you want. Soba shops and small family restaurants in the countryside also have counter seats or small tables for one.

Tips for eating alone in Japan

1. Say “hitori desu” (one person) and show one finger at the door.

2. Choose counter places: ramen, soba, sushi, curry, gyudon, teishoku (set meals).

3. Don’t stay too long at busy, small shops when people are waiting. Eat, say “gochisousama deshita,” and go.

4. Busy at lunch? Try a little before 12:00 or after 13:00.
