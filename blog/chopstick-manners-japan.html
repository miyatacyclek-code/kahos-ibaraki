Chopstick manners in Japan: the rules nobody tells you

by Kaho · Ibaraki, Japan

Videos about Japanese eating manners get millions of views online. And the most surprising part for many people is this: some chopstick rules in Japan are not about being polite. They are about death.

Don’t worry. Nobody expects visitors to be perfect. But if you know the “why,” the rules are easy to remember.

The two big ones

1. Don’t stand your chopsticks up in a bowl of rice. This is called tatebashi. In Japan, a bowl of rice with chopsticks standing in it is an offering for someone who has just died. So at a normal dinner table, it looks like a funeral. If you need a break, lay them down.

2. Don’t pass food from your chopsticks to someone else’s chopsticks. This is called hashiwatashi. After a cremation in Japan, family members pick up the bones with chopsticks and pass them to each other, then put them in an urn. Passing food the same way reminds people of that moment. If you want to share, put the food on the other person’s plate.

The small everyday rules

Japanese people call bad chopstick habits kiraibashi (嫌い箸), “disliked chopsticks.” Here are the ones you will see most:

3. Mayoibashi: moving your chopsticks around over the dishes because you can’t decide what to eat. Decide first, then reach.

4. Sashibashi: stabbing food like a fork. If something is slippery, it’s okay to use a spoon or just try again.

5. Yosebashi: pulling a plate or bowl closer with your chopsticks. Use your hand.

6. Pointing at people with your chopsticks. Same as pointing with a finger, but a little worse.

Why it matters so much

In Japan, chopsticks touch your mouth, so they are very personal. Many families even have their own chopsticks for each person at home. Because of that, how you use them says a lot about how you were raised. Grandparents in the countryside often teach these rules to children at the dinner table.

And here’s one thing that is NOT rude: lifting your rice bowl or miso soup bowl close to your mouth. In Japan, that is normal and good manners.

Tips for visitors

At restaurants with shared plates, use the serving chopsticks if there are some. When you rest, put your chopsticks on the little chopstick rest (hashioki), or across the paper wrapper. And at a sushi counter, it’s fine to eat nigiri with your fingers.
