Overtime in Japan: what it's really like

by Kaho · Ibaraki, Japan

One of the most common questions I get: “Do Japanese people really work that much?” The short answer: it depends on the company. But overtime culture is real. Japan even has a word, karoshi, for death from overwork. Here is my honest view as a working person in Japan.

Why people stay late

  • Leaving before your boss can feel awkward.
  • Teamwork culture — if your team is busy, you help, even if your own work is done.
  • Lots of meetings and paperwork (yes, we still love hanko stamps).
  • In some companies, staying late used to look like “working hard,” even if it wasn't efficient.

Service overtime

In the past, many people did “service zangyo” — overtime that is not paid. It is illegal, but it happened a lot. Today companies are much stricter about recording working hours, but you still hear stories.

What is changing

Things are slowly changing. In 2019, the “work style reform” law started to set legal limits on overtime. The basic limit is 45 hours a month and 360 hours a year, and companies that break the rules can be punished. Before that, there was almost no real limit.

  • More companies let people work from home, at least sometimes.
  • Flex time is more common, so you can start and finish earlier or later.
  • Many younger people care more about work-life balance, and they change jobs if the company is too “black” (that's what we call bad companies).
  • Taking paid holidays is easier than before. Companies now have to make sure workers use at least 5 days of paid leave a year.

City vs countryside

My life is in Ibaraki, not Tokyo. Here most people drive to work, so there is no crowded train at midnight. Some people say the pace is a little slower. But long hours exist everywhere, in the city and in the countryside.

After a long day, a convenience store dessert is my therapy.

How I survive

Weekends in nature. That's why I love Ibaraki — one drive and I'm at the ocean or a waterfall. Then on Monday I'm ready again (kind of).

Tips if you want to work in Japan

  • Ask in the interview about average overtime hours. It's a normal question now.
  • Check if overtime is paid by the minute or if there is “fixed overtime” (minashi zangyo) in your contract.
  • Look at reviews from employees online before you join.
  • Learn a few polite phrases for leaving on time, like “osaki ni shitsurei shimasu” (excuse me for leaving first).
