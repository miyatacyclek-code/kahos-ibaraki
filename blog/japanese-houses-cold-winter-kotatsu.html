Why Japanese houses are so cold in winter

by Kaho · Ibaraki, Japan

Many people who visit Japan in winter say the same thing: "Why is it colder inside the house than outside?" It sounds like a joke, but in an old countryside house it can feel true. Here in Ibaraki, winter nights are often close to 0°C, and many homes do not have central heating.

Heat the person, not the house

In most Japanese homes, you heat only the room you are using. An air conditioner, a gas or kerosene heater, maybe an electric carpet. The hallway, the toilet and the bathroom stay cold. Many people think it is a waste to warm empty rooms, and older wooden houses were built for hot, humid summers, not for keeping heat in.

So the idea is simple: keep your body warm. Thick socks, slippers, layers, and one very special table.

The kotatsu (炬燵)

A kotatsu is a low table with a small electric heater under the tabletop. You put a thick futon blanket between the frame and the top, sit on the floor, and put your legs inside. Only your lower body is in the heat, but somehow your whole body feels warm.

It started long ago with the irori, a sunken fireplace with charcoal. People put a wooden frame and a quilt over the heat. Electric kotatsu became common in the 20th century, and they are much safer than charcoal. Some restaurants and izakaya still have a hori-gotatsu, where the floor is dug out so you can sit like on a chair.

The kotatsu is also where the family gathers in winter. Mikan oranges on the table, TV on, nobody wants to stand up. In Japan we joke that once you get in, you can never leave.

The cold bathroom problem

The cold part of the house is not just uncomfortable. When you go from a warm room into a cold changing room and then into a hot bath, your blood pressure changes quickly. In Japan this is called "heat shock", and it is a known risk for older people in winter. That is why the government tells people to warm the changing room first, keep the bath water at 41°C or lower, and not soak for more than about 10 minutes.

Tips for visitors in winter

Pack warm socks and layers you can take off, because trains and shops are warm but homes are not. Buy kairo, disposable heat packs, at any convenience store. If you stay at a ryokan or a farm stay in the countryside, ask if they have a kotatsu. And at an onsen, do not jump straight into very hot water. Go slowly.
