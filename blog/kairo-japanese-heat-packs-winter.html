Kairo: Japan’s little pocket heaters for winter

by Kaho · Ibaraki, Japan

In winter, you will see them everywhere in Japan: small white packs in pockets, inside school uniforms, and even inside shoes. They are called kairo (カイロ), and for many Japanese people, winter without them is hard to imagine.

What is a kairo?

A kairo is a disposable heat pack. You open the bag, the pack touches the air, and in a few minutes it gets warm. Inside there is iron powder. When the iron meets oxygen, it slowly rusts, and that makes heat. Many packs stay warm for most of the day.

The word is written 懐炉. 懐 (futokoro) is the space inside the front of your clothes, near your chest. 炉 means furnace. So kairo is a “furnace you keep inside your clothes.”

From warm stones to konbini shelves

The idea is old. In the Edo period, people heated a stone, wrapped it in cloth and put it inside their kimono. This was called onjaku (温石). Later came kairo that burned ash, and then ones that used benzine gas.

The big change came in 1978, when a disposable pack called Hokaron went on sale. You just shook it and it got warm. In 1989 a stick-on type arrived. Today, December 1 is even “Kairo Day,” chosen by the industry in 1991.

A small furnace you keep inside your clothes.

Why Japan loves them

Many Japanese houses are not well insulated, and people often heat one room, not the whole house. Trains and offices are warm, but platforms, shrines and walks outside are cold. A kairo is cheap, light and works anywhere.

In the countryside, like here in Ibaraki, winter mornings are often below zero. Farmers, students waiting for the bus, and people watching the winter sunrise at the sea all know the small joy of a warm pocket.

Tips for visitors

You can buy kairo at any konbini, drugstore or supermarket from autumn. There are three main types: harenai (貼らない) to hold in your hand or pocket, haru (貼る) to stick on your clothes, and small ones for your shoes or socks.

Stick them on your clothes, never on your skin. Do not use them while you sleep. Even a warm pack can cause a low-temperature burn if it stays in one place for a long time. Your lower back or the back of your neck (over your shirt) are popular spots.
