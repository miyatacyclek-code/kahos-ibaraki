Eating while walking in Japan: rude or not?

by Kaho · Ibaraki, Japan

You buy a hot snack on a Japanese street, take a bite, and keep walking. Then you notice something. Most Japanese people around you are not walking. They are standing next to the shop, eating quietly.

This is one of the most talked-about “unwritten rules” in videos about Japan. So is eating while walking really rude here? The honest answer: it depends on where you are.

What is tabearuki?

In Japanese, eating while walking is called tabearuki (食べ歩き). It is not illegal anywhere in Japan. Nobody will give you a fine. But many people, especially older people, feel it is a little bad-mannered in everyday places like a busy station, a train platform or a quiet shopping street.

Why do Japanese people stop to eat?

The main reason is meiwaku (迷惑), the idea of not causing trouble for others. On a crowded street, sauce or ice cream can drip on someone’s clothes. Sticks and wrappers can end up on the ground, and Japan has very few public trash cans.

There is also a quieter reason. Many Japanese people feel food should be enjoyed properly, sitting or standing still, not eaten in a hurry. It is the same feeling behind saying “itadakimasu” before a meal.

Tourist towns feel this too. In 2019, Kamakura, near Tokyo, started asking visitors not to eat while walking on its crowded shopping streets. It is a polite request, with no penalty.

When it is totally fine

At a matsuri (festival), everyone walks around with yakisoba, grilled squid or chocolate bananas. In Ibaraki, festival nights and fireworks nights are full of food stalls, and eating on the go is part of the fun. Some sightseeing streets are even famous for tabearuki, and the shops sell snacks on sticks made to be carried.

Long-distance trains are another exception. Eating an ekiben (station bento) on the shinkansen is one of the joys of travel in Japan. On a crowded local commuter train, though, most people do not eat.

Tips for visitors

Eat right next to the stall or shop where you bought the food. Many shops have a small bench or a spot to stand. Keep your trash until you find a bin, or give it back to the shop that sold you the food. At a convenience store, look for an eat-in corner. And if you see a sign saying not to eat while walking, just follow it.

You don’t have to be perfect. Japanese people know travelers are learning. A small effort is always noticed, and always appreciated.
